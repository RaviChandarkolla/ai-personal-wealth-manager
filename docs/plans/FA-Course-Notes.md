## Introduction

### What is Fundamental Analysis (FA)?

- Fundamental analysis is a study of assessing stock from a 360 degree perspective.
- It combines analysis of everything about a company.
- The study can be of internal factors affecting the company like operational efficiencies, management competence, or usage of technology.
- Or it can be of external factors affecting the company like geo politics, supply chain disruptions, industry trends, or government policies.

### What are the types of FA?

- **Qualitative**
- **Quantitative**

### Myths around FA

1. Investing in Stock market is nothing but Gambling.
2. I can surely make money with the help of short term news.
3. We need to wait for the market to fall before investing – Buy Low Sell High.
4. Stock market is the best investment.
5. I am from non-finance background, how can I generate returns against smart people from finance background?

### How to do Fundamental Analysis?

- **Economy Analysis**
- **Industry Analysis**
- **Company Analysis**

## Economy Analysis

Key economic indicators to study:

- GDP (Gross Domestic Product)
- IIP (Index of Industrial Production)
- PMI (Purchasing Managers Index)
- Inflation
- CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)
- SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)
- Repo Rate
- Reverse Repo Rate
- Unemployment Rate
- Infrastructure Development
- Political Stability

### GDP (Gross Domestic Product)

- It is the sum of the total final output (goods and services) of a country.
- Higher the output, better for the country - more production implies more demand - more demand indicates more employment - demand in other sectors grows - the economic cycle runs smoothly.
- GDP at constant price (meaning adjusted for inflation) should be looked at to see the real increment in the GDP.
- There is no good or bad number for GDP. It should be generally on an increasing trend, indicating healthy growth in the economy.
- Reference: https://tradingeconomics.com/india/gdp-constant-prices

### IIP (Index of Industrial Production)

- IIP is a Composite index. It measures the volume of industrial production of a basket of industrial products such as Electricity, Steel, Refinery products, Crude Oil, Coal, Cement, Natural Gas and Fertilizers, etc. during a given period - as compared to a base period.
- IIP will increase when there is more demand - demand implies more employment - production is increasing - economy will flourish.
- There is no good or bad number for IIP. It should be increasing, indicating healthy growth.
- Reference: https://www.mospi.gov.in/web/mospi/download-tables-data/-/reports/view/templateFour/23802?q=TBDCAT

### PMI (Purchasing Managers Index)

- PMI is an indicator of the economic health. It is a survey based index that summarizes the answers received on various aspects from senior executives of various companies.
- This exercise is done by IHS Markit – A division of S&P Global.
- Difference between IIP and PMI is that IIP is actual data which is compiled and published by the Government, whereas PMI is a survey based index by Private companies.
- An index value above 50 indicates a positive development whereas a value below 50 indicates a negative situation. A value of 50 represents no change as compared to the previous period.
- Reference: https://tradingeconomics.com/india/indicators

### Inflation

- Inflation refers to a general increase in prices of goods and services in an economy. A healthy inflation of 4-6% per annum is good for the economy and is desirable, but high inflation is not good for the economy.
- The correlation between the economy and inflation has to be understood:
  - If all the indicators like GDP, PMI, IIP etc. are stagnant or falling and inflation is rising, then it means that inflation is rising because of supply side issues and is not good.
  - If all the indicators are rising and inflation is rising, then it means that inflation is rising because of higher demand and hence is not problematic for the economy.
- Reference: https://tradingeconomics.com/india/indicators

### CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)

- CRR is a cash reserve that the banks have to maintain with the RBI. The RBI Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) changes this rate timely to maintain the money flow in the economy.
- Increasing CRR – Less money with banks to lend – Interest rates will rise as money becomes dearer – Capex and consumption will be halted – Slowdown.
- Normally CRR is kept at 4%, however, in the times of hardship like Covid, it is reduced to increase the money supply (During the pandemic it was kept at 3%).
- Reference: https://tradingeconomics.com/india/indicators

### SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)

- SLR is a reserve that the banks have to maintain with themselves, in the form of cash, gold reserves, government bonds or other securities.
- Increasing SLR – Less money with the banks to lend – Interest rates rise as money becomes dearer – Capex and consumption will be halted – Slowdown.
- Normally SLR is kept above 20%, however, in the times of hardship like Covid it is reduced to increase money supply (Since the pandemic it is kept at 18%).
- References:
  - https://www.rbi.org.in/Home.aspx
  - https://www.numberbasket.com/india/finance/rbi-key-rates/statutoryliquidity-ratio-slr

### Repo Rate

- Repo rate is the rate at which the banks take loan from RBI.
- If it increases, banks will take less money from RBI - Less money with banks – Interest rates will rise – Capex and consumption will be halted – Slowdown.
- From 2018, the repo rate is fluctuating between 5% to 6.5%. In the times of hardship like Covid, it is reduced to increase money supply (Since the pandemic it is kept at 4%).
- References:
  - https://www.rbi.org.in/Home.aspx
  - https://freefincal.com/rbi-repo-rate-history/

### Reverse Repo Rate

- Reverse repo rate is the rate at which the RBI takes money from the banks.
- If it increases, banks will park more money with RBI - Less money with banks - Interest rates will rise - Capex and consumption will be halted - Slowdown.
- From 2016, the Reverse Repo Rate is fluctuating in between 5 to 6.5%. In the times of hardship like Covid, it is reduced to increase money supply (Since the pandemic it is kept at 3.35%).
- Reference: https://www.rbi.org.in/Home.aspx

### Unemployment Rate

- When people are unemployed, they have no source of income, hence there is less demand in the economy.
- Less demand leads to less production - layoffs in less contributing departments - economy going towards a recession.
- This data is based on directly interviewing a large sample of randomly selected households and is published by CMIE, which is a privately owned database company.
- Reference: https://unemploymentinindia.cmie.com/kommon/bin/sr.php?kall=wshowtab&tabno=0001

### Infrastructure Development

- High Capex on infrastructure inflicts confidence amongst investors and attracts private investments, which leads to creation of employment leading to higher demand, all in all resulting into sustainable and stable economic growth.
- The details about the Capex can be found in the Union budget.
- One can google about the Government Capex expenditure and various news articles etc. will have this information readily available along with comparisons with the previous years.
- Reference: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YxdaXPMvOoA

### Political Stability

- Political stability refers to a stable government which has a majority. It allows them to take decisions without interference from other political parties.
- A government with majority can take decisions fast and without any intervention from other parties. There is no delay in the implementation of various economic policies and reforms resulting into faster economic growth of the country.

### Impact of General Elections on Sensex

| Year | Outcome |
|------|---------|
| **1991** | Congress won the elections and formed the government. During this tenure they did lot of reforms and it attracted lot of foreign investors. |
| **1996-98** | India saw 3 PMs during this time. As govt had no stability, Indian market struggled with Asian financial crisis during this time and hence lack of confidence was evident among investors. |
| **1999** | NDA came in power. This was the first non-INC government to complete the full term. But market suffered due to global factors & the dot com bubble in just a few months post elections. |
| **2004** | Congress back in power. Market fell 15% in first week after election results. And then resumed its rally. |
| **2009** | Congress once again won the elections. Market which was on the path to recovery after the 2008 Financial Crisis gained 17% in single day. Big gap up! |
| **2014** | NDA came in power with full majority. Riding on high hopes from the government about reforms and stability, market did well post elections. |
| **2019** | NDA came back to power with full majority. Reforms and stability was again the main reason behind markets doing well post elections in 2019 until the pandemic hit. |

## Industry Analysis

- It is the analysis of an industry as a whole. We will have to study whether the industry as a whole is doing good and expected to do good in the coming times or not.
- For that we must be aware of the industry type: **Cyclical** or **Non-cyclical** (Generally also known as Defensives).
  - **Cyclical** industries are the ones whose share prices closely follow the economic activities.
  - **Non-cyclical** are the ones whose share prices are not much dependent on the economic activities.

### Industry Stage & Future Anticipation

- One more thing to understand is the stage in which the industry currently is. For example, in 2000, the internet companies were at a very nascent stage and the people who anticipated that internet is going to be the next big thing and invested in IT companies at that time, made a fortune.
- Banking, Credit cards, etc., even though looking to be matured in India, still have a long way to go as working population is increasing and is expected to increase consistently.
- You will have to anticipate the future of the industry and bet on it. Say currently when there is a fad about EV all around, you can't make it big by investing in auto companies which are laggards about investing in the EV eco system.

### Government Policies & PLI Schemes

- You see that the Government policies are in the favor of EV companies.
- Government is giving subsidies for buying EV's. So it may be a prudent decision to invest in an EV company as the political intent is supporting it.
- The Government intent can be ascertained through the PLI schemes as well.
- Govt gives various types of incentives/subsidies for manufacturing certain products in India. For example, there are PLI schemes for Auto components, Automobiles, Aviation, Chemicals, Electronic systems, Semi conductors, Medical devices etc.
- Reference: https://www.investindia.gov.in/production-linked-incentives-schemesindia

### Qualitative Analysis

#### Financial Statements

- Financial statements are a representation of the financial position and performance of an entity. The objective is to provide information that is useful to a wide range of users in making economic decisions.
- Financial statements also show the results of the management's utilization of the resources entrusted to it. To meet this objective, financial statements provide information about an entity's:
  - Assets
  - Liabilities
  - Equity
  - Income and Expenses, including gains and losses
  - Contributions by and distributions to owners in their capacity as owners
  - Cash flows

Balance sheet is a statement reporting a company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time. It provides a picture of what a company owns and owes, as well as the amount invested by shareholders.

#### Notes to Accounts

- Notes to Accounts is supporting information provided along with the company's financial statements.
- It helps to understand the computation of figures appearing in the financial statements.

- Notes are important for users of the financial statements as they may reveal issues with a company's financial health.

## Ratio Analysis

Ratio Analysis is a quantitative method in which absolute figures are converted in uniform ratios to compare the performance of the company over time and of one company with another.

Common ratio categories:
- Activity Ratios (also known as Turnover Ratios)
- Liquidity Ratios
- Solvency Ratios
- Profitability Ratios
- Valuation Ratios

### Activity Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Inventory Turnover | COGS / Average Inventory |
| 2 | Receivables Turnover | Revenue / Average Receivables |
| 3 | Payables Turnover | Purchases / Average Trade Payables |
| 4 | Working Capital Turnover | Revenue / Average Working Capital |
| 5 | Fixed Asset Turnover | Revenue / Average Net Fixed Assets |
| 6 | Total Asset Turnover | Revenue / Average Total Assets |

### Liquidity Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Current Ratio | Current Assets / Current Liabilities |
| 2 | Quick Ratio | (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities |
| 3 | Cash Ratio | (Cash + Short-term marketable investments) / Current Liabilities |
| 4 | Defensive Interval Ratio | (Current Assets - Inventory) / Daily Cash Expenditure |

### Solvency Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Debt-to-Asset Ratio | Total Debt / Total Asset |
| 2 | Debt-to-Capital Ratio | Total Debt / (Total Debt + Total Shareholders' Equity) |
| 3 | Debt-to-Equity Ratio | Total Debt / Total Shareholders' Equity |
| 4 | Financial Leverage | Average Total Assets / Average Total Equity |
| 5 | Debt-to-EBITDA | Total Debt / EBITDA |
| 6 | Interest Coverage | EBIT / Interest Payments |
| 7 | Fixed Charge Coverage | (EBIT + Lease Payments) / (Interest + Lease Payments) |

### Profitability Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Gross Profit Margin | Gross Profit / Revenue |
| 2 | PBT Margin | PBT / Revenue |
| 3 | PAT Margin | PAT / Revenue |
| 4 | ROA (Return on Assets) | Net Profit / Average Total Assets |
| 5 | Return on Capital Employed | EBIT / Capital Employed |
| 6 | Return on Equity | Net Profit / Average Total Equity |

### Valuation Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | P/E | Price Per Share / Earnings Per Share |
| 2 | P/CF | Price Per Share / Cash Flow Per Share |
| 3 | P/S | Price Per Share / Sales Per Share |
| 4 | P/BV | Price Per Share / Book Value Per Share |

### Per Share Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Basic EPS | (Net Profit – Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Number of Shares outstanding |
| 2 | Cash Flow Per Share | Cash Flow from Operations / Weighted Average Number of Shares Outstanding |
| 3 | EBITDA Per Share | EBITDA / Weighted Average Number of Shares Outstanding |
| 4 | Dividends Per Share | Dividend / Weighted Average Number of Shares Outstanding |

### Dividend / Growth Ratios

| Sr. No | Ratio | Formula |
|--------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Dividend Pay-out Ratio | Dividend / Net Profit attributable to equity shareholders |
| 2 | Retention Ratio | 1 – Dividend Pay-out Ratio |
| 3 | Sustainable Growth Rate | Retention Ratio × ROE |

#### Interest Income

- Interest income is the main source of income for banks.
- If the interest income is rising at a good pace, it means that the bank is growing its business in a healthy way.
- However, if the interest income is decreasing / not increasing at a similar rate as compared to its competitors, then it indicates that the competitors are eating up the market share.

#### NII (Net Interest Income)

- NII is the difference between interest earned by banks from lending activities and the interest paid by the banks on deposits taken.
- If NII is increasing, it means that the interest income is outpacing the interest expense and hence is good for the bank.
- However, if NII is decreasing, it means that the margin of the bank is contracting and is a caution point for investors as it directly impacts the PAT.

#### NIM (Net Interest Margin)

- NIM is the Net Interest Income (absolute interest income – absolute interest expense) expressed as a % to the total loan book of the bank.
- Example: A bank advances a loan of Rs. 1 crore; interest of Rs. 11 lakhs earned. Money procured at interest of Rs. 5 lakhs. NIM = (11,00,000 - 5,00,000) / 1,00,00,000 = 6%.
- A higher NIM is better for the bank as it suggests that the interest margin is healthy.

#### NPA (Non Performing Assets)

- NPAs are loans given by the bank that the bank is unable to recover (E.g.: Kingfisher).
- It is expressed as a % of total loans given. Banks are required to make provisions in respect of NPAs. Lower the NPA, better it is.
- **Gross NPA:** normal NPA, calculated as a percentage to the advances given. It is the real measure to analyze the quality of the loans given by the bank / recovery efficiency of the bank.
- **Net NPA:** Gross NPA – (Interest and Principal recovered + Provisions made + Money insured by bank (e.g. DICGC)). Used for investment decisions.

#### Cash Flow Analysis

- Generally, cash flows from operations for any company should be positive.
- However, in the case of banks, this doesn't hold true.
- This is because the main business of a bank is to give loans to customers. As money goes out from the bank while giving a loan, cash flow becomes negative.
- Is giving a loan a bad sign for the bank? Not at all! If the cash flow is negative, it means the bank has given a lot of loans, and hence it is actually good for the bank.
